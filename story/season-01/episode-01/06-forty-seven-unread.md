---
title: Forty-Seven Unread
pov: Ananya
date: Wednesday, 25 February 2026
place: Northstar Institute of Technology, Indore
---

Sunlight. The ceiling fan turning slowly, the way it has turned every morning for four years.

Ananya is asleep face-down with one arm hanging off the bed when her phone starts buzzing, and she ignores it, because it is Wednesday and her first class is at eleven and she has not attended it since November.

It buzzes again. And again. Not the long pattern of a call. The short, stacked pattern of many messages landing at once, like hail on a tin roof.

She opens one eye. Reaches. Squints.

> **47 UNREAD MESSAGES**

She scrolls. The family group. The placement group. The hostel group. A group she muted in second year and has never once read. All of them, all this morning.

Sid has sent her a video at 3:12 a.m. It's the international news anchor from last week, the polished one, mid-sentence. Then the feed stutters. Freezes. For half a second the anchor is a still photograph with her mouth open. Then colour bars, a tone, and a slate:

> **WE ARE EXPERIENCING TECHNICAL DIFFICULTIES**

The tone holds. Nobody comes back. Sid has added a caption: *BRO THE NEWS JUST DIED 💀*

Below it, a forward from someone in the placement group with nineteen fire emojis, a Telegram link promising *REAL FOOTAGE FROM DELHI HOSPITAL NOT FOR WEAK HEARTS*, and a long message that begins *As per my cousin who is in the army* and that she does not read.

And a clip, shaky, filmed at night from a car: a snowy highway, and people walking down the centre line in the headlights. Twenty or thirty of them, all at the same pace, all turning their heads at the same moment toward the camera. Somebody in the car is swearing in French. The caption says *ontario rn??*

She swipes past it, fast, the way you pull your hand off a hot pan.

Wedged between them, an email notification:

> **NEXORA HR:** Your joining date remains unchanged. We look forward to welcoming you on 1 June.

She almost laughs. Then she opens the family group.

> **MOM:** ARE YOU AWAKE
> **MOM:** CALL
> **MOM:** ANANYA
> **MOM:** PICK UP THE PHONE

She sits up fast and calls. It rings, and rings, and connects.

"— why aren't you answering, we've been calling since six, your father has been —"

"Mom. Mom. I'm fine. I was asleep."

"You need to come home."

"I have three months of college left."

"Ananya."

Something in the way her mother says it. Not angry. Her mother is angry all the time, it's practically a dialect. This is something else, something flat and careful. Ananya goes still.

"What's happening?"

A pause on the line, full of the sound of the house in Goa: a pressure cooker, the neighbour's dog, the sea, very faint, if she's imagining it.

"Have you looked outside?" her mother asks quietly.

Ananya turns to the window. She hasn't. She does now.

* * *

At first glance the campus is normal. Students. Bikes. The chai stall by the main road, steam going up from the kettle.

But it's wrong in small ways, the way a room is wrong when somebody has moved one piece of furniture.

Nobody is walking alone. People move in clumps of three and four, heads down, phones up.

There's a queue outside the ATM by the admin block. A long one, longer than she has ever seen it, the kind of queue that only forms when a lot of people have decided at the same moment, without telling each other, that they might need cash.

The chai vendor isn't watching his kettle. He's watching a phone propped against the sugar tin.

A boy runs across the lawn. Not late-for-class running. The other kind.

Ananya walks out of the hostel still on the phone. "It looks fine," she tells her mother. "It looks completely —"

She stops.

On the notice board by the main gate there is a fresh printout. The tape is still curling at the corners. A small crowd is reading it in silence.

> **CAMPUS ADVISORY: IN-PERSON CLASSES SUSPENDED UNTIL FURTHER NOTICE. STUDENTS ADVISED TO REMAIN ON CAMPUS. — OFFICE OF THE DIRECTOR, IN CONSULTATION WITH THE DISTRICT ADMINISTRATION**

"Mom," Ananya says, "I'll call you back."

She hangs up before her mother can argue, which is a thing she has never once in her life managed to do.

* * *

The mess hall has long steel tables, a smell of poha and floor cleaner, and half its usual crowd.

The television in the corner is showing a government press briefing on mute. A Health Ministry official at a podium with a row of microphones. Behind him, a screen of helpline numbers. A line of text across the bottom:

> **STAY INDOORS. AVOID NON-ESSENTIAL TRAVEL. REPORT FEVER AFTER ANY INTERNATIONAL OR DOMESTIC AIR TRAVEL.**

Nobody is watching it.

Sid is sitting with a tray he hasn't touched. Aditya is across from him with his laptop open, reading fast, saying nothing.

Ananya drops onto the bench beside them.

"Somebody explain this to me," she says, "in one sentence."

Aditya doesn't look up. "Nobody can. That's the problem."

"They cancelled classes," Sid offers. "Which is technically a win, if you think about —"

"Sid," Ananya and Aditya say together.

"Okay. Reading the room."

Aditya turns the laptop toward her. A map, but not a news map — a crowdsourced one, messy, unofficial, pins dropped by thousands of strangers, the kind that moves faster than the official ones. There are pins across the country now.

"These aren't confirmed," Aditya says. "Half are probably panic. Some are definitely fake. But the pattern's wrong."

"Wrong how?"

"We keep looking at it like a map. Like it spreads by distance." He zooms out. The pins aren't near each other. They aren't near Canada. "It doesn't. Look. Toronto. London. Frankfurt. Dubai. Delhi. Mumbai."

He taps each one.

"It spreads by flights. Every one of these is a major airport. It didn't crawl here." He zooms back in until the map fills with the familiar grid of Indore, the ring road, the airport on the western edge of the city. There's a pin on it. Then another, closer, on the road to campus. "It flew."

Nobody says anything for a moment.

The Health Ministry official on the silent television turns a page.

"I liked it better," Sid says finally, "when it was space dust."
