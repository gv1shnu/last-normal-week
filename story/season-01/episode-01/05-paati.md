---
title: Paati
pov: Kamala, then Priya
date: Sunday, 22 February – Monday, 23 February 2026
place: Highway 69, Ontario; Delhi
---

The airport shuttle leaves Sudbury at half past one in the morning, because Kamala's flight leaves Toronto at noon and the man at the shuttle company told her, twice, that the 69 is no joke in February.

She has been ready since eleven. Her silver hair is in its bun. Her sari is ironed under her winter coat, which her son says is ridiculous and which she says is how a person travels. On her lap is a cloth tote of gifts she has packed and unpacked four times: two sweaters she knitted herself, a box of maple candies shaped like leaves, a toy moose that sings when you press its stomach. She left India thirty-four years ago. Her grandchildren in Delhi are nine and six, and she has held them only on a phone screen.

Tomorrow she will hold them properly.

The van is half full. A student asleep against the window. A man in a hard hat with his steel-toed boots up on the seat. The driver plays country music very low and drives carefully, and the headlights push a short tunnel through the dark, and outside there is nothing but black spruce and snow and the reflective posts ticking past.

Kamala does not sleep. She never sleeps on the way to anything.

Somewhere south of a town whose name she misses, the driver says, "What the —"

People. In the road. Standing on the centre line in the headlights, a lot of them, more than she can count, and not moving out of the way.

The driver brakes. The van slides on the packed snow, slews, straightens. Too late to stop, too late to turn.

The van noses into them.

A thud, then another, bodies against the bumper and the hood. The van rocks. Then there are hands on the glass. Faces at every window, grey under the dome light, mouths slack, eyes dark and wrong and calm. Nobody screams except the people inside.

Kamala's window caves in.

Cold air and glass and a hand, and the hand closes on her left arm through the open front of her coat, and a mouth finds the soft inside of her forearm above the glove.

She screams. She has not screamed since she was a girl.

The driver floors it. The tyres spin, catch, and the van tears free, bodies sliding off the hood, the hand ripped away, and then they are moving, fast, faster than is safe, the broken window howling.

Behind them, in the red of the tail lights, the figures do not chase. They stand in the road and watch the van go, all of them, together.

Then there is only the wind through the window and the driver on the phone to 911, shouting a kilometre marker, and the student crying, and Kamala's own breathing, very loud.

She looks down at her arm.

A bite. Deep. A ring of torn skin, bleeding slowly, and already, at the edges, darkening.

She presses her palm over it. She takes the scarf from her neck and wraps it, tight, the way her mother taught her to wrap a cut from the coconut knife.

"Ma'am? Ma'am, are you hurt?" the man in the hard hat says. "Ma'am — your window —"

"I'm fine," Kamala says. "The glass. Only the glass."

She says nothing else for the rest of the drive. At Pearson, the airport police take everyone's names and the driver's statement, and a young officer asks her again if she was hurt, and she says again that she was not. Her flight is at noon. Her grandchildren are waiting.

She pulls the sleeve of her blouse down over the scarf and goes to check in.

* * *

Thirteen hours and twenty minutes over the top of the world.

She doesn't eat. She can't get warm. Twice she goes to the tiny bathroom and unwraps the scarf and looks, and wraps it again. The black has spread beneath the skin in thin threads, like ink dropped in water. Her arm aches to the shoulder. Her head is full of a slow, heavy roar.

When the plane comes down through the brown afternoon haze over Delhi, she is grey and upright by sheer will.

The arrivals hall at Indira Gandhi International is the ordinary roar of a huge airport. A thermal camera on a tripod sweeps the queue. On its screen her face shows up in yellows and oranges, a shade hotter than the man in front of her. Not hot enough. The officer waves her on.

And there, beyond the barrier, are two children holding a sign in felt pen with too many exclamation marks.

Aarav, nine, solemn, trying to look grown up.

Meera, six, who forgets to be anything at all.

"*Paati!*" [Grandma!]

They crash into her. For one moment Kamala forgets the arm, the roar, the faces at the window. She smiles, and it's the first real thing she has felt since the road.

"Look how big," she says softly, in Tamil and then in English, because she doesn't know which one they'll understand. "Look how big you are."

She holds them both. With her right arm only.

* * *

The flat in Mayur Vihar is warm and noisy and smells of sambar and frying appalams. The gifts are open. The moose has sung its song eleven times. A cup of filter coffee sits in front of Kamala, going cold, untouched.

Her daughter Priya watches her from across the table.

"*Amma* [Mom], you're grey. Have you eaten anything?"

"Long flight."

"Let me see your —"

Priya reaches for the left arm. Kamala pulls it back. Too fast.

"I'm fine."

A pause. Priya looks at her the way Kamala used to look at Priya when she was fifteen and lying.

So Kamala stands, to prove it.

The room tilts very gently, like a boat. The floor comes up to meet her, kind and slow. She hears cups shatter. She hears Meera scream.

Then nothing.

* * *

The emergency department is fluorescent and loud and working. Priya stands in the doorway of the resuscitation bay because nobody has told her to leave yet, and she understands, distantly, that this is a good hospital, that the system is doing exactly what it is meant to do: a triage nurse, a trolley, a doctor in under four minutes.

The doctor is young. He cuts away the left sleeve of her mother's blouse with trauma shears. Then he unwinds the scarf.

The bite is black.

Not bruised. Black, the colour of something burnt, and from it dark lines run up the inside of the arm under the skin, branching like the roots of a tree.

"When did this happen?" the doctor says. "Ma'am — can you hear me? Aunty?"

Her mother's eyes are half open and see nothing.

On the monitor the heart rate is climbing. 120. 150. 180.

"What is that?" Priya hears herself say. "What is that on her arm?"

Nobody answers. Someone is calling for a senior. Someone is drawing blood. The number on the monitor spikes, and holds, and then the line begins to fall, and falls, and goes flat.

The long, flat tone.

"Start compressions," the doctor says, already climbing onto the step beside the trolley, his hands locked on her mother's breastbone. The charge nurse is at the crash cart.

"Adrenaline, one milligram —"

Her mother's hand closes on the doctor's wrist.

He freezes mid-compression. The tone does not stop.

Kamala's eyes open fully. The whites have gone the colour of a bruise.

She sits up. The wrong way.

And in a bright, crowded emergency room, in the middle of a city of thirty million people, it starts.
