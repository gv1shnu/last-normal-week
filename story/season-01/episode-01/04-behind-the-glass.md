---
title: Behind the Glass
pov: Dr. Claire Bouchard, then none
date: Friday, 20 February – Sunday, 22 February 2026
place: Sudbury and Northern Ontario, Canada
---

The isolation room was built for tuberculosis and rehearsed for Ebola, and Dr. Claire Bouchard has never seen it used for either. Tonight it holds a geologist named Martin Lavoie, thirty-six, who yesterday morning flew into the bush in a helicopter to look at a rock.

She watches him through the observation glass. Negative pressure hums in the vents. Her own breath is loud inside the powered respirator hood, and her gloved hands are folded in front of her because there is nothing useful left for them to do.

Martin is in restraints. He wasn't, until the evening, when he stopped recognising the nurses and started trying to stand on a body that could no longer hold him. Fever sweat soaks the pillow. His lips have gone grey. His chest works far too hard for the air it pulls in, and the oxygen saturation on the monitor sits in the low eighties no matter what they give him.

Three of the survey team are on this floor. Martin came in first and is going fastest.

Beside her, Dr. Anand, the intensivist, reads the monitor without blinking.

Heart rate 120.

140.

160.

"He's going," Anand says quietly.

Martin's eyes open. The whites have gone the colour of a bruise, purple-red, as though every vessel in them burst at once. He isn't looking at anything.

The rate climbs past 180, touches 190, and then the trace begins to fall — not settling, falling — 90, 60, 40. The QRS complexes widen and slur and spread apart.

Then nothing. A flat line. A long, flat tone.

"Asystole," Anand says. "Going in."

They move together for the anteroom, the way they have drilled a hundred times, already reaching for fresh gloves, already counting in their heads: compressions, epinephrine, look for a cause.

Claire glances back at the glass.

Martin's hand is closing. Slowly. One finger at a time.

She stops. Anand stops beside her.

The tone goes on. The trace isn't quite flat anymore; it's jittering, a ragged scribble of noise. Claire knows what it is before she lets herself think it. Movement artifact: the signal from skeletal muscle contracting under the electrodes. Not the heart. Underneath the noise, there is no rhythm at all. The pulse oximeter has no waveform. His chest does not rise.

He has no pulse and he is not breathing, and his head turns toward the glass.

He sits up against the restraints, but not the way a person sits up. The joints lead and the body follows, as if something is working the frame from the inside, learning where the hinges are.

His mouth opens.

The sound that comes out is low and wet and, horribly, almost musical.

*HNNNNG…*

"That's not possible," Anand whispers.

Martin's bruise-coloured eyes find them through the glass, and hold.

Neither of them says the word, because there isn't a word. Claire has a medical degree, two fellowships, and twelve years in critical care, and she stands there in a hood that sounds like the sea, searching every one of them for a name for what she is looking at, and finds nothing.

* * *

At eleven that night the anchor on the late national bulletin speaks a little faster than she did on Thursday.

"Officials have confirmed that four tourists who entered the restricted forest area before the exclusion order are currently unaccounted for."

A photograph: four hikers in a parking lot, grinning, snowshoes strapped to their packs. Ordinary people on an ordinary winter trip.

"Authorities are asking residents in nearby communities to report any unexplained illness or unusual behaviour."

> **HIKERS MAY HAVE LEFT RESTRICTED ZONE**

The anchor pauses. One hand goes to her earpiece. Her expression changes, only slightly, and only for a moment.

"We have —" She listens. "We have received an update. One of the missing hikers has been located."

* * *

Sunday, a little after two in the morning. A town on Highway 69 with a gas station, a Tim Hortons, and a motel with half its sign lit.

Minus eighteen. The snow along the shoulders is blue under the streetlights.

A man stands under one of them. Barefoot in the snow. Blood dried brown down the front of his jacket. His head tilted slightly upward, as if listening to something far away.

Across the road, a second figure stands just as still.

The man makes a sound. Low. Wet. Almost musical.

*HNNNNG…*

The second figure answers.

*HNNNNG…*

The man turns, slowly. Not toward the road. Toward the dark behind the motel.

More figures come out of it. One. Three. Ten. Twenty. Some in parkas, some in pyjamas, one still in a gas-station uniform with a name badge. They don't rush. They don't wander. They move together, the way a flock turns.

The man makes another sound, and the others answer, one after another down the line, until the sound travels the length of the empty road like a conversation nobody understands.

Then, all together, they walk out onto the highway and turn south, down the centre line, toward Toronto.
