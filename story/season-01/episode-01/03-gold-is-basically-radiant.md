---
title: Gold Is Basically Radiant
pov: Arjun
date: Friday, 20 February 2026
place: Northstar Institute of Technology, Indore
---

The shuttle comes over the net slow and high, the way Kabir always serves when he's tired and pretending not to be.

Arjun Reddy lets it drop. He waits one beat longer than feels safe, steps in, and snaps his wrist.

The smash goes past Kabir's left hip so fast that Kabir only turns his head after it's gone. It clips the back line. White on white.

"In," Arjun says. "Point. Game."

"The floor's uneven on my side."

"It's the same floor."

"Then it's the lighting."

Arjun doesn't answer. He walks to the back of the court, picks up the shuttle, and smooths its feathers between two fingers. Three games to nothing. Kabir has never once taken a game off him, and has never once stopped explaining why.

The indoor hall is the only building on campus where Arjun feels tall. He is twenty, quiet, from Vijayawada, and most days he moves through Northstar the way you move through a city where you half know the language — which is exactly what he's doing. Telugu is home. Hindi he learned in school from Class 6, out of a textbook, as a subject with marks attached. On a good day he follows about half of what's said around him. He smiles at the other half. People take the smile for agreement and keep going.

On the court there's nothing to translate.

Kabir Singh collapses onto the bench and drinks half a bottle of water in one go. He's twenty-one, built like he plays every sport badly and enthusiastically, and dressed, as always, as if February were June. He's from Indore itself. His mother sends tiffin to the hostel twice a week, and he guards it like a dragon.

"Fine," Kabir says. "You own this court. *Lekin* [But] tonight — ranked — I drag your Iron ass out of the pit like always."

"Gold isn't the flex you think it is."

"In this hostel?" Kabir points at him with the bottle. "Gold is basically Radiant."

Arjun doesn't dignify it.

Through the open double doors of the hall, the evening is turning orange. Someone's playing music by the hostels. A volleyball goes up over the sagging net on the lawn and comes down in the wrong place.

A girl walks past the doorway. Tall — taller than Arjun — with a black T-shirt, a ponytail, and the unhurried walk of someone who has nowhere she needs to be and is going there anyway.

Kabir is on his feet. "Ma'am!"

She stops. Turns. Looks at Kabir with an expression Arjun has seen on professors looking at late assignments.

"Don't call me ma'am."

"Sorry, ma'am."

She looks at him a second longer, decides he's not worth the energy, and walks on.

Kabir sits back down, delighted with himself.

"Who is she?" Arjun asks.

"Final year."

"That's it?"

"CSE. Placed. Rich." Kabir takes another swig. "And hot — which you clearly noticed."

Arjun ignores the last part, which is the safest thing to do with most of what Kabir says. "How do you know she's rich?"

"She has a MacBook."

Arjun glances at his own phone on the bench. The screen is a spider web of cracks radiating from one corner, where it met the floor of a Vijayawada bus stand two years ago.

"That doesn't mean rich."

Kabir considers this seriously.

"For us," he says, "it does."

They both laugh.

* * *

After dinner the common room is full, which means the television is on, which means the meteorite again.

Arjun hates the news. He has hated it since he was fourteen and realised that every channel his father watched was the same three men shouting about the same three things in slightly different shirts. It's doom and political theatre, all of it. He doesn't need it to know the world is complicated.

But tonight the coverage isn't Indian. It's an international feed, the one the hostel warden likes, and the pictures are different. Snow. Satellite tracks of the fireball, a bright smear across a weather map. Scientists in parkas. A police cordon across a forest road, officers in masks holding a line behind yellow tape — *DO NOT CROSS* — and reporters kept well back, their microphones straining toward the trees.

Masks, Arjun thinks. Why masks, if there's no radiation?

"Authorities have expanded the exclusion area," the reporter says, "after several members of the initial survey team reported unusual respiratory symptoms."

The strip at the bottom of the screen changes.

> **THREE SCIENTISTS HOSPITALIZED**

The room goes a little quieter. Someone says, in Hindi, something Arjun catches only the shape of — *bimar* [sick] — and somebody else answers with a joke he doesn't catch at all, and half the room laughs.

Then someone grabs the remote and finds the cricket, a commentator mid-sentence, a stadium roaring, and the whole room relaxes into it like a warm bath.

Arjun doesn't.

He doesn't know why. The story has nothing to do with him. It's twelve thousand kilometres away, in a country where it's snowing. But something about the masks sits wrong, and when something sits wrong it stays with him, the way a sound stays when you're trying to sleep.

He takes out his cracked phone and opens the story himself, which he never does.

> **HIKERS MAY HAVE LEFT RESTRICTED ZONE**

He reads it twice. Four tourists, backcountry permits, last checked in two days before the impact. Search teams. A phone number.

The phone buzzes in his hand. *Amma* [Mom] on the screen.

> **AMMA:** DON'T GO ANYWHERE ALONE THIS WEEK.

He frowns.

> **ARJUN:** WHY?

The reply comes quickly. His mother has never typed quickly in her life.

> **AMMA:** NEWS.
> **AMMA:** CALL ME LATER.

He looks up at the television.

The bowler is running in. The commentator's voice climbs, the stumps go cartwheeling, and the common room erupts — forty boys on their feet, shouting, slapping each other's backs, Kabir somewhere in the middle of it with both arms in the air.

The meteorite disappears under the noise.

Arjun sits very still in the middle of the celebration with the phone in his hand, and for no reason he can explain, counts the doors.
