# The Last Normal Week — Series Bible

The backbone of the series: premise, world rules, cast, and structure. Every chapter must agree with this file. When the story changes something here, update this file in the same commit.

## Premise

A group of college students lives through the ten weeks between an outbreak's arrival in India and the summer heat that might end it, while a government that refuses to fall fights to hold the line until May. Staying alive turns out to be easier than deciding what kind of society deserves to survive.

## Series identity

This is not a collapse story. Most apocalypse fiction is about institutions vanishing; this one is about how a country stands up to an extinction-level event and whether it can hold for ten weeks. Four pillars set it apart:

1. **The apocalypse has a season.** The infected cannot survive sustained heat, and Central and North Indian summer arrives in May. The goal is not a cure or a safe zone; it is to hold until the heat, then survive what the monsoon brings back.
2. **The state stands up.** The Indian government does not fall. It mobilises, contains, triages, and makes hard calls under impossible pressure. The drama is the strain of doing that, and the people caught on the wrong side of a necessary rule, not the absence of authority.
3. **The feed never goes dark.** Mobile networks stay up. Reels, group chats, rumours, and livestreams run through the whole story as its chorus, spreading misinformation as fast as the pathogen spreads.
4. **Normal life refuses to stop.** Joining dates, rescheduled exams, parents on the phone, and placement anxiety keep running alongside the outbreak. The dry comedy of people acting as if the old plans still apply is part of the tone.

## Format

The series is written as prose fiction and published on the web. It is organised like a television season so it can be adapted later:

- **Season** — a complete arc with its own title. Season 1 runs from the impact in February to the first monsoon rain.
- **Episode** — a self-contained stretch of story with its own title, theme, and visual identity on the site. An episode is several chapters.
- **Chapter** — one page on the site. Usually one point-of-view character, one stretch of time, roughly 1,500 to 3,500 words.

How to write the prose is in [style.md](style.md). What each episode covers is in [season-01.md](season-01.md).

## Setting and timeline

The story opens on **Thursday, 19 February 2026**, with a meteorite falling into the boreal forest of Northern Ontario, Canada.

The main setting is **Northstar Institute of Technology**, a fictional Tier-1 engineering campus on the outskirts of **Indore, Madhya Pradesh**. Indore sits on the Malwa plateau, about 560 metres above sea level. Late February there is the end of a mild winter: warm days around 29°C and cool nights around 11°C. In May the average high is about 40°C, which puts the campus inside the heat clock. Indore has an airport with domestic links across India and international flights to the Gulf (Devi Ahilya Bai Holkar Airport), so the outbreak can reach it directly.

Ananya, Sid, and Aditya are in the final semester of a B.Tech program, roughly three months from a May graduation, with placements already concluded. Arjun and Kabir are juniors.

## Language

English is the base language. Dialogue may code-switch into Hindi, Telugu, Tamil, Konkani, Marathi, or Meitei for realism. Wherever it does, the original words are kept and an English translation follows in square brackets, so every line stays readable.

## Tone

Grounded, tense, character-driven post-apocalyptic drama with thriller mechanics. Avoid treating the apocalypse as spectacle for its own sake; the pressure should come from scarcity, interpersonal fracture, moral choices, and institutions straining to hold. Officials, doctors, police, and soldiers are people doing a hard job, shown with respect, not villains or fools. Dry comedy from normal life refusing to stop is welcome; it should never undercut the danger.

## Ensemble

The central group should contain distinct academic, social, economic, and personality backgrounds. Each character has a practical capability that matters during the crisis and a personal liability that creates conflict.

These are ordinary undergraduates, not action heroes or savants. No one improvises satellite uplinks, engineers cures, or builds tech from scratch. Their edges are mundane and human — local knowledge, physical fitness, a level head, nerve, loyalty — and their survival comes from decisions, not gadgets.

## World rules

- **Scientific accuracy.** Every geographic, biological, medical, and physical detail must match mainstream scientific consensus and be checked against reliable sources before it goes into a chapter. The only invented science is the pathogen itself and what it does to the body; everything around it (how meteorites fall, how hospitals treat cardiac arrest, how outbreaks travel, real places, climates, and populations) behaves as it does in reality.
- **The meteorite fall follows real physics.** Small impactors almost always arrive unseen: the surveys are built for far larger objects, and only about a dozen objects have ever been spotted before impact. The fireball is bright enough to see in daylight and goes dark high up; the surviving fragment falls the last several kilometres unlit at terminal speed and lands cold, not hot: no steam, no crater, just a small pit, with frost forming on its crust. Its cold interior is what let something survive the trip. The site is found the same day from fireball tracking data and documented by drone.
- **The catastrophe is biological.** A "behavioral pathogen" seeded by the meteorite, not a kinetic or nuclear event.
- **The Indian government stands up.** State institutions, security forces, health services, and telecom remain functional throughout. The response draws on real precedents from India's COVID-19 response: powers under the Epidemic Diseases Act, 1897 and the Disaster Management Act, 2005, containment zones, sealed campuses, thermal screening, a contact-tracing and health-status app in the spirit of Aarogya Setu, and evacuation flights in the spirit of the Vande Bharat Mission. Once the heat effect is understood, the national strategy becomes explicit: contain, protect the plains, and hold until May. Breakdowns are local, temporary, and caused by overload, not collapse. Governments in colder countries fare worse, which makes India a destination rather than a ruin.
- **The heat clock.** The pathogen is a cold-origin organism and breaks down when its host stays above roughly 40°C for days; the damage is cumulative and does not reverse when the host cools. The infected have no pulse, no breath, and no sweating, so like any body without circulation they drift toward the temperature around them and heat further in direct sun; heat also speeds their decay. This one threshold is invented. Everything else follows real climate data:
  - May mean maximums are about 39.5°C in Delhi, 40°C in Indore, and 42°C in Nagpur, so the interior plains become hostile to the infected in May.
  - Goa (about 33°C in May), Imphal (about 29°C), and hill towns such as Shimla (about 23°C) never get hot enough. Ananya's and Sid's homes stay dangerous, which pulls against the group's safety in the heat.
  - The monsoon reaches Kerala around 1 June and Delhi around 27 June, and its cloud and rain bring temperatures down. The heat window closes as the monsoon advances.
  - Nobody knows any of this at first. It is discovered mid-season by observation: the infected start moving into shade, basements, cold storage, and air-conditioned buildings as spring warms. The cool, dark places survivors hide in are the places the infected start wanting.
- **The feed stays up.** Phones, mobile data, and social platforms keep working, as essential services did in 2020. Characters always have information; the problem is that most of it is wrong, late, or performed for views.
- **Nobody guesses "virus."** The public reads the meteorite as an alien event — memes, jokes, "aliens before GTA 6" — and nobody's mind jumps to a pathogen or the walking dead. The misread is deliberate and persists: the truth arrives faster than anyone's framing for it, and the word for what's happening does not exist yet within the story.
- **Two-stage infection.** Stage one: the mist from the split meteorite is the original vector. It infected only those physically at the impact site (the survey scientists and the four hikers), by inhalation, which is why the first symptoms are respiratory. Stage two: human-to-human spread is by bite and bodily fluids; an infected person must attack to pass it on. There is an incubation window of about one to two days, so the exposed can move, and fly, before they turn. Modern outbreaks spread along flight routes rather than by geographic distance, so early cases cluster around major airports.
- **Two populations from patient zero.** The scientists are contained, hospitalized behind glass; their scenes exist to show the transformation, not to spread it. The hikers are the loose vector — unprotected, gone from the zone before the cordon, seeding the outbreak in the towns.
- **Behavior of the infected: coordinated and lethal, not frenzied.** They don't sprint or thrash. They move together, purposefully, communicating through a low, wet, almost-musical sound; deadly on contact but eerie and controlled. The fear is the collective — they hunt as one — which is why "don't run alone" is survival, not a mood.
- **A pack, not a hive mind.** The infected coordinate like wolves: signals, timing, movement. There is no shared consciousness, no speech, no agenda, and no attempt to persuade or assimilate anyone. The sound is never explained.
- **No real people in invented scenes.** Real journalists, politicians, and public figures never appear speaking invented lines. Use fictional anchors, officials, and institutions; real places, laws, and organisations are fine.

## Season architecture

Season 1 follows the calendar from late February to the arrival of the monsoon, in three movements.

1. **The fracture (late February to early March).** The ordinary campus world fractures. The outbreak reaches the campus, the gate is chained, and the protagonists converge on a defensible refuge. The state's first response is visible and imperfect; the feed is louder than the facts; Nexora still expects Ananya on her joining date.
2. **The long hold (March to April).** The group establishes rules, searches for resources, encounters other survivors, and learns that cooperation has costs. The campus becomes a sealed zone inside a functioning but overloaded state: screening, lists, rationing, and the question of whether to report a bitten friend. As spring warms, the infected begin to seek the cool, dark places, including the group's own refuge, and the heat effect is discovered. The national strategy becomes "hold until May." Home pulls at them: Goa and Imphal will never get hot enough, and Kabir's family is a short drive away.
3. **The heat (May to the monsoon).** Peak summer turns the plains against the infected, and the hold starts to work, but the infected concentrate in every cool place left, and the monsoon is coming. A major external threat forces the group to choose between preserving itself and risking everything to protect a larger community. The season ends as the first rain arrives and nobody yet knows what it brings back.

Later seasons are open.
